Behaviour has improved fast. Belonging hasn't kept pace.

Pupils describe behaviour far more positively than a year ago. The share who feel they belong has not risen with it. What the national survey shows, what it cannot, and what I have seen make the difference.

Mark DaltonFirst published on LinkedIn, 18 November 2026Sources

Secondary pupils in England are describing behaviour in their schools far more positively than a year ago. In May 2025, 39% of Key Stage 3 pupils and 44% at Key Stage 4 rated it good or very good. By July 2026, 55% and 68% did.11DfE, Parent, pupil and learner voice: June 2026, published 10 November 2026; fieldwork 30 June to 14 July 2026. Key Stage 3 n = 883, Key Stage 4 n = 682. May 2025 comparators from the same release. Over the same period, the share who felt they belonged at school on most days fell at Key Stage 3, from 72% to 66%, and barely moved at Key Stage 4. Calm corridors matter for belonging. They are not the same thing, and the national data now shows the gap.

What the survey shows

The Department for Education published its latest Parent, pupil and learner voice survey on 10 November. The headline was a fall in belonging at Key Stage 3 and among 16 to 19 year olds, and a fall in enjoyment at every key stage. At Key Stage 4, fewer than half of pupils (49%) had enjoyed going to school on most days in the past week.

The same release shows behaviour ratings rising sharply, and more Key Stage 4 pupils saying rules were applied fairly all or most of the time (65%, up from 58%). Pupils are describing behaviour more positively. That is encouraging, even if the survey cannot tell us precisely what has driven the change.

Read the timing with care

This year's fieldwork ran from 30 June to 14 July, later in the term than last year's. Within this school year, Key Stage 3 belonging was 79% in March and 66% by July. The release does not discuss that change, and it has not been tested statistically. The department itself says comparisons between years are less reliable than comparisons within a year.22DfE, Parent, pupil and learner voice: March 2026 (Key Stage 3 n = 1,319; fieldwork 11 March to 7 April 2026) and May 2025 (fieldwork 21 May to 13 June 2025).

It also helps to look back one more year. The department's National Behaviour Survey found that the share of pupils in Years 7 to 13 who felt they belonged on most days rose from 57% in May 2024 to 69% in May 2025, a statistically significant increase.33DfE, National Behaviour Survey: findings from academic year 2024 to 2025, published 20 November 2025. Pupils in Years 7 to 13. This year's figures, at 65% to 66% in each key stage, are lower than last year's but still well above 2024's.

By the last fortnight of term, Year 11 have left, examinations have changed how parts of the building are used and attention is already turning towards the next September intake. Younger pupils and staff are tired, and transition activities and changes to the usual rhythm can make school feel different from the place pupils knew in March. That does not prove the fall is seasonal, but it is a good reason to be cautious about treating differently timed surveys as directly comparable.

The later timing did not stop behaviour ratings rising. Before anyone builds policy on a six-point fall in belonging, though, I would like to see the question asked at the same point every year, with the pattern within the year published alongside it.

Belonging does not need to earn its place through grades

There is a temptation to sell belonging as a lever for attainment. Two careful studies this year suggest caution.

John Jerrim, writing for FFT Education Datalab on 2 November, linked international surveys to the National Pupil Database. In Year 9, a one-standard-deviation difference in belonging was associated with almost half a GCSE maths grade. Once prior attainment, background, attendance and exclusions were taken into account, that fell to 0.16 of a grade, and in Year 11 it disappeared.44John Jerrim, The link between school belonging and GCSE outcomes, FFT Education Datalab, 2 November 2026. The department's own linked study of PISA 2022, published in August, found no significant link between the six-item OECD belonging index and Attainment 8, and only a modest one for a single direct question about belonging.55DfE, School belonging and attainment at key stage 4 (RR1645), 19 August 2026. 4,763 pupils in 165 schools, PISA 2022 linked to the National Pupil Database. Neither study tests an intervention, so neither shows that belonging cannot help learning.

What they do show is that belonging should not have to justify itself through GCSE outcomes. A child who wants to be in school, who is known by name and who believes the adults are on their side is a good in itself. It is part of what a school is for. Programmes sold on the promise of higher grades risk being judged, and dropped, on the wrong test.

Where the gaps sit

The national levels move around. The gaps between groups appear whichever survey you use.

The department's Growing Up in the 2020s study surveyed nearly 8,000 Year 8 pupils in 2025. Of those, 63% agreed they belonged at their school. That was 57% for girls against 69% for boys, 57% for pupils eligible for free school meals against 66% for others, and 54% for pupils with special educational needs against 66%.66DfE, Growing Up in the 2020s: first survey of young people at age 12 and 13 (RR1654), 1 October 2026; fieldwork January to June 2025. The PISA-linked study, the department's own pupil survey and the #BeeWell surveys find gaps in the same direction, although each asks a different question. In PISA 2022, 63% of 15-year-olds in England said they belonged at school, against an OECD average of 75%.77PISA 2022: national report for England, December 2023.

The #BeeWell programme surveyed about 40,000 pupils in 253 schools. Only around 3% of the variation in belonging lay between schools; most lay among pupils in the same school.88#BeeWell, Evidence briefing: school belonging, March 2025. Autumn 2023 survey; 3.17% of the variation lay between schools. That does not mean the rest is within a school's control, because pupils bring very different lives through the gate. The strongest correlate was pupils' relationships with staff. The research is observational, so it cannot show that those relationships cause belonging, but it does suggest where a leader might look first.

What I have seen make the difference

In the schools I have led and visited, behaviour and belonging develop together rather than one after the other. Children cannot feel at home in a building where they do not feel safe. But I have also seen calm become an end in itself, with routines that are tight and relationships that are thin.

This is not a case for weakening expectations. Predictable routines are often one of the ways a school shows pupils that they matter. The difference lies in whether those routines sit alongside fairness, recognition and adult trust, or whether compliance becomes the whole measure of success.

Fairness that pupils can see. In my experience, the difficult part is not writing a clear behaviour policy. It is making sure pupils experience that consistency, from one classroom to the next, and that adults will explain a decision and listen if they have got something wrong. We have also had to think carefully about how reasonable adjustments are made: fairness does not always mean treating every pupil identically, but an unexplained exception can look very much like favouritism to the child sitting beside them.

Being known by an adult. I put considerable weight on the daily tutor relationship. A settled start to the morning, with time for reading and a familiar adult, creates the opportunity to notice who has returned after an absence, who is unusually quiet or who needs someone to follow up. For pupils facing greater barriers, that needs to connect with more individual support. The test is not whether we can point to a named adult on a spreadsheet, but whether the pupil would name that person themselves.

Something to belong to beyond lessons. A house system and opportunities for leadership can help, but I have become more interested in who does not take part. The pupil who never volunteers, never joins a team and rarely receives recognition needs a way into the life of the school too. Belonging cannot be reserved for the children who already have the confidence to put themselves forward.

What would help

Three changes would make the national survey far more useful to schools.

First, ask the belonging question at the same point every year, and publish the pattern within the year alongside the comparison between years.

Second, publish the results by free school meal eligibility, special educational needs and sex for every wave. The survey is already weighted on all three.99DfE, Parent, pupil and learner voice: technical report, 2025 to 2026 academic year, November 2026.

Third, encourage schools to ask their own pupils the same short question at regular points. Once a term is enough to see a pattern without wearing pupils out. A school that asks in the same words over time will learn more about its own pupils than any national average can tell it.

Pupils are telling us that behaviour feels better. If calm is now the floor, what are you measuring to know whether your pupils feel the school is theirs?
