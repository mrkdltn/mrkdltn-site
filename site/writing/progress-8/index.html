Progress 8: a lens refracted by context

Why the return of Progress 8 in 2027 should prompt a serious national conversation about progress, context and equitable school accountability.

Mark DaltonFirst published on LinkedIn, 20 September 2026Updated 15 November 2026Sources

Introduced nationally in 2016, Progress 8 represented an important shift in secondary accountability. It zoomed out from a narrow focus on headline attainment thresholds and gave much greater weight to the progress pupils make from their starting points. That was a sensible shift. But attainment still matters too. Schools should care about both how far pupils travel and where they eventually arrive.

The pandemic interrupted that picture. With KS2 assessments cancelled, official Progress 8 could not be calculated for the 2025 and 2026 examination cohorts. Its return in 2027 therefore gives us a useful opportunity to look again at the measure: what it has improved, what it still misses, and how fairly it reflects achievement across schools serving very different communities.

The question is not whether progress should matter. It should. Nor is it whether attainment should matter. It should too. The challenge is making sure both are interpreted with enough care, context and precision to support fair evaluation of schools.

The current Year 11 cohort is the first since the pandemic disruption for whom KS2 prior-attainment information is available again, so Progress 8 can once more be calculated for the 2026/27 results. DfE guidance confirms the return of the measure for this cohort.

At the same time, Progress 8 is moving back into a more consequential accountability landscape. Ofsted’s September 2026 toolkit places greater emphasis on achievement in comparison with national averages and similar schools, while government has consulted on using progress data as part of the route into Targeted RISE support from January 2028. The new Ofsted toolkit is now in use.

Update, 15 November 2026

Three things have moved since this was published. The provisional 2025/26 results, released on 15 October, again carry no Progress 8 score. They show the disadvantage gap index narrowing to 3.82 from 3.92 a year earlier, though it remains wider than the 3.70 recorded in 2018/19 (DfE, Key stage 4 performance 2025/26, provisional).

The government response to the key stage 4 consultation has still not been published. As at 15 November the consultation page says only that feedback is being analysed.

And a full year of Ofsted report cards now shows how much rests on the achievement grade: 46% of secondary schools serving the most deprived fifth of neighbourhoods were graded below Expected, against 8% in the least deprived fifth. I set that out in I checked every Ofsted report card.

Does Progress 8 really control for Prior Attainment?

At pupil level, Progress 8 explicitly adjusts for prior attainment. A pupil with high KS2 scores is compared with pupils nationally who had similarly high KS2 scores. A lower prior attainer is compared with other lower prior attainers.

So the simplistic argument that schools receive a Progress 8 advantage merely by admitting higher-attaining pupils is wrong. The more interesting question is whether, once those pupil-level adjustments have been made, schools with higher-attaining intakes still tend to record higher Progress 8 scores.

Historically, they have. Cambridge Assessment’s national analysis found a school-level correlation of around 0.33 between mean prior attainment and Progress 8. The same analysis also found negative relationships between school P8 and FSM, SEN and deprivation. Cambridge Assessment’s analysis remains a useful description of the structural issue.

That study used early Progress 8 cohorts, so it should not simply be read across to 2027. The more recent national pupil-group data, however, still shows sizeable systematic differences after prior attainment has already been accounted for.

The national data tells a more unequal story

In 2023/24, disadvantaged pupils averaged -0.57 while non-disadvantaged pupils averaged +0.16. Pupils receiving SEN support averaged -0.45, and pupils with an EHCP -1.13. EAL pupils averaged +0.51, compared with -0.13 for non-EAL pupils. At broad ethnic-group level, White pupils averaged -0.15, Mixed pupils -0.02, Black pupils +0.26, Asian pupils +0.51 and pupils in the Other category +0.53. These figures come from the DfE’s 2023/24 KS4 performance release.

These groups overlap substantially and should not be turned into predictions about individual pupils. They do, though, show that matching pupils on KS2 starting point does not remove large differences in later outcomes.

Two pupils can have the same prior attainment and still face very different patterns of later achievement. Progress 8 does not model every factor associated with those differences.

Disadvantage is difficult to ignore

The national P8 difference between disadvantaged and non-disadvantaged pupils in 2023/24 was 0.73 of a grade per subject: -0.57 against +0.16. At the same time, the wider disadvantage gap is not closing. The Education Policy Institute’s 2026 annual report estimates that disadvantaged pupils were 19 months behind by the end of secondary school in 2025, up from 18.8 months in 2024 and a full month wider than before the pandemic. EPI’s 2026 disadvantage report also identifies absence as a major part of the post-pandemic widening.

Attendance is plainly something schools influence. Strong schools improve it, and poor attendance cannot become a blanket explanation for weak outcomes. Yet attendance is also shaped by poverty, health, SEND, housing, family circumstances and access to wider services. A pupil who misses a significant proportion of secondary education has had less access to curriculum and teaching. Progress 8 records the examination outcome; it does not record the amount of education available to produce it.

The disadvantaged White British pattern

DfE published an ad-hoc analysis of the 1,228 secondary schools where more than 20% of pupils completing KS4 in 2023/24 were both disadvantaged and White British. Only 21 schools had a Progress 8 score above zero for their disadvantaged White British pupils — around 1.7%. The DfE data can be explored here.

That does not mean 98.3% of those schools had negative overall P8. Nor should ‘disadvantaged White British’ be casually substituted for ‘white working class’; one is a specific DfE classification and the other a broader socioeconomic description. Even with those caveats, the national pattern is stark. If almost every school serving a substantial population from one disadvantaged group records a non-positive progress outcome for that group, the system should be curious about how much of that pattern is attributable to schools and how much reflects influences not captured by prior attainment alone.

SEND, low prior attainment and the scoring basket

The 2023/24 national P8 average for pupils with SEN support was -0.45; for pupils with EHCPs it was -1.13. One mechanism sitting underneath those figures is the likelihood of having fewer than eight scoring slots.

DfE’s technical work for the 2026 consultation found that around 14% of pupils overall had fewer than eight scoring slots in the underlying analysis. Among low prior attainers the figure was around 33%; among low-prior-attaining pupils receiving SEN support around 44%; among low prior attainers with any SEN around 49%; and among low-prior-attaining pupils with an EHCP around 66%. The figures are set out in the consultation technical guide.

The educational judgement is not straightforward. Schools should protect access to a broad, ambitious curriculum and should be wary of reducing entitlement to improve accountability outcomes. There will also be pupils for whom eight scoring qualifications are not the right programme. The arithmetic cannot distinguish between unnecessary curriculum narrowing and a carefully designed programme for a young person with significant need. In both cases, an empty slot scores zero.

Empty slots and Progress 8 outcomes

DfE’s modelling shows how strongly slot completion is associated with P8. Among low prior attainers, pupils filling eight slots averaged around +0.57 compared with -1.16 for those with seven or fewer. For middle prior attainers the equivalent figures were +0.29 and -2.28; for high prior attainers, +0.14 and -3.04. The same technical guide reports these patterns.

This also corrects a common misconception about higher prior attainers. Their P8 calculation is not inherently easier. Their expected Attainment 8 is high, so missing a scoring slot can be particularly damaging. The more useful concern is that schools serve very different mixes of disadvantage, SEND, attendance, mobility, curriculum completion and qualification entry, while the formal P8 adjustment remains based principally on prior attainment.

The national system has already moved towards contextual comparison

A major change this September is the introduction of the DfE and Ofsted similar-schools comparator. Every mainstream secondary is now matched with around 50 statistical neighbours to help leaders and inspectors interpret attainment in context.

For secondary schools, the model uses KS2 prior attainment, FSM6, POLAR4, SEN support, IDACI deprivation, pupil stability, EHCP prevalence, EAL and school size. Prior attainment receives the largest weighting, but it is far from the only factor. DfE’s technical note sets out the full methodology, and reports that the model explains around four-fifths of the variation in secondary-school attainment.

The principle is worth dwelling on. For interpreting attainment, the national system now formally recognises that prior attainment, disadvantage, deprivation, SEND, EAL and pupil stability all shape the context in which outcomes are produced. Progress 8, by contrast, makes its formal adjustment through prior attainment. The measures serve different purposes, but that difference should make us cautious about treating P8 as if it were a context-neutral estimate of school effectiveness.

Ofsted 2027/28: where the risk sits

The September 2026 inspection toolkit directs inspectors to use published outcomes as a starting point, to consider performance over time, and to compare with both national averages and similar schools. It also asks inspectors to recognise the limitations of published data and to test the picture through first-hand evidence: pupils’ work, learning, discussions and case sampling. The schools inspection toolkit is available here.

Ofsted’s May 2026 commentary goes further, warning that national averages never give the full picture and that the term ‘typically’ is deliberate because national results may not fully describe achievement in every context. Ofsted’s commentary on achievement and challenging circumstances.

The practical risk in 2027/28 is less about an explicit rule that ‘negative P8 equals weak school’ and more about the gravitational pull of a clean, comparable national number. After two years without a current P8 score, a fresh figure will arrive and it will be easy for achievement conversations to orbit around it.

Schools serving high proportions of disadvantaged pupils, low prior attainers, pupils with SEND, pupils with historically poor attendance or high mobility will need to know exactly what sits underneath their headline score. That is not special pleading. It is the analytical work required to judge achievement properly.

What is confirmed for 2027, and what remains proposed?

The clearest confirmed point is that Progress 8 can return for the summer 2027 cohort because usable KS2 prior-attainment data exists again.

The Government has also proposed a redesigned P8 curriculum model, retaining eight qualifications but restructuring the basket around English, double-weighted mathematics, two science slots, two breadth slots and two choice slots. The proposed model is intended for pupils taking examinations in 2028/29, not the summer 2027 cohort. The consultation sets out the proposed model and implementation timetable.

The consultation also proposes an additional ‘best-fit’ progress measure for low prior attainers. Rather than always calculating progress across eight qualifications, it would compare several possible basket sizes and use the pupil’s strongest result. Headline P8 would remain in place. FFT Education Datalab’s modelling found a very strong relationship between the existing low-PA P8 measure and the proposed best-fit version, suggesting that it may improve treatment of some pupils without fundamentally changing the school-level picture. FFT’s analysis of the best-fit proposal.

FFT has also modelled the proposed future Attainment 8/P8 curriculum changes and found that 92% of schools would have remained within 0.1 P8 points of their existing 2024 result. Its modelling of the proposed curriculum reform is here.

The policy timetable is now a problem in its own right

The consultation stated that the Government response would be published in the summer term 2026 so that schools could plan subject choices for pupils beginning GCSE study in September 2027. At the time of writing, on 20 September 2026, the official consultation page remains closed without a final government response shown there. The consultation page can be checked here.

Schools therefore need to keep a clean line between confirmed policy and consultation proposals. Progress 8 can return in 2027. The redesigned curriculum model is proposed for 2028/29. The low-prior-attainment measure remains a proposal. The consultation’s plan to use progress data in Targeted RISE eligibility from January 2028 also remains proposed rather than settled.

RISE raises the stakes

The consultation proposes using an Attainment 8 threshold initially, because there is no current P8 measure for the pandemic cohorts, and then moving towards progress data from January 2028. It also recognises that schools with high proportions of pupils with additional needs may be more likely to trigger a threshold, and suggests professional discretion where a school is already performing well in context or clearly improving. The RISE proposals are contained in the same consultation.

Once a measure is used not simply to describe outcomes but to determine intervention, the validity of the inference made from it becomes more important. A P8 score tells us something important about pupil outcomes. Whether the same number, by itself, can reliably identify which institutions need external improvement support is a separate question.

A better national conversation

We do not need to choose between high expectations and contextual understanding. Nor do we need to choose between standardised national comparison and local explanation.

The similar-schools model offers a useful principle. Keep the national attainment measure, then add a second lens: how does this school perform compared with schools educating statistically similar populations? That comparison can expose weak performance more sharply just as easily as it can reveal exceptional performance in a challenging context.

The same principle could inform progress. Standard P8 should remain visible. Alongside it, a contextualised analytical measure could help leaders, trusts, inspectors and government test whether the headline result is unusually strong or weak once pupil composition is considered. It should be a diagnostic lens, not an excuse and not another league table.

What should be reviewed nationally?

  • Publish standard P8 alongside a transparent contextualised analytical measure, rather than expecting one number to carry both functions.
  • Keep statistical uncertainty visible. Cohort size, confidence intervals and multi-year trends should remain prominent even if percentile bands are introduced.
  • Make intersectional analysis easier. PP, SEND, prior attainment, ethnicity, mobility and attendance do not occur in separate children.
  • Evaluate the proposed best-fit low-prior-attainment measure in shadow form before attaching high-stakes consequences to it.
  • Use the first post-pandemic P8 cohort in summer 2027 for an independent fairness review before progress data becomes more deeply embedded in intervention and accountability.
  • State clearly across DfE, Ofsted, RISE and trust accountability that Progress 8 is an outcome measure, not a direct school-effect estimate.

Context cannot become an excuse

A disadvantaged pupil does not need a system which quietly lowers expectations because disadvantaged pupils have historically achieved less. A pupil with SEND does not need adults assuming that academic success belongs to somebody else. A pupil arriving in Year 7 significantly behind does not need their starting point to become their destination.

Schools serving challenging communities need high ambition, strong teaching, a demanding curriculum and relentless work on attendance. None of that requires crude evaluation of institutions.

If we want excellent teachers and leaders to work in the communities where educational inequality is greatest, the system needs to distinguish between a school perpetuating low achievement, a school improving rapidly from a very low base, and a school producing exceptional outcomes in difficult circumstances. Raw attainment cannot do that well enough. Progress 8 gets much closer. The similar-schools approach adds another useful lens. None of them tells the whole story.

Progress 8 should start the conversation, not finish it

The return of Progress 8 is welcome. School leaders should understand the measure extremely well: the basket, subject contribution, prior-attainment groups, disadvantage, SEND, attendance, mobility, empty slots, cohort effects and the pupils who sit behind the average.

Then we should look beyond the number. What do pupils actually know? What can they do? Are those who started furthest behind catching up? Are disadvantaged pupils accessing the same ambitious curriculum? Are pupils with SEND achieving well rather than simply receiving provision? Are young people leaving with qualifications and destinations that expand their choices?

Progress 8 contributes to those judgements. It cannot make them on its own.

Progress 8 attempts to control for prior attainment. It does not control for context, and it should not be treated as a direct estimate of school effectiveness.

As the measure returns in 2027, there is a genuine opportunity to strengthen the national conversation: retain the virtues of Progress 8, become much more precise about its limits, and make sure that fairness and equity sit alongside ambition in the way we evaluate schools.
