Curriculum & Assessment Review: questions every school leader should be asking now

What the Review asks of leaders in practice: the behaviours that make change stick, the technical planning behind it, and the questions worth asking now.

Mark DaltonFirst published on LinkedIn, 5 November 2025Updated 15 November 2026Sources

The 2025 Curriculum and Assessment Review marks a sizable shift in England's education system. Led by Professor Becky Francis, the review calls for a more inclusive, ambitious, and future-facing curriculum. While many headlines have focused on broad themes like the EBacc measure or enhancing oracy (important), school leaders must now grapple with a deeper question: what does this mean for leadership in practice?

Update, 15 November 2026

I wrote this on the day the Review was published. A year on, the timetable is clearer, and it has slipped once. The government responded the same day and confirmed a revised national curriculum for first teaching from September 2028, with the final programmes of study due in spring 2027 (DfE, government response).

The public consultation on the draft programmes of study was promised for September 2026. In October, Tes reported that it had been put back to later in the year. Each week of delay shortens the time schools have between the final curriculum and first teaching in September 2028.

On teacher supply, the 2025/26 training census recorded secondary recruitment at 89% of target, up from 61% the year before. Computing reached 81%, physics 78% and design and technology 71% (DfE, Initial Teacher Training Census 2025/26). The consultation on Progress 8 and Attainment 8 that followed the Review closed on 4 May 2026 and still awaits a response, which I look at in Progress 8: a lens refracted by context.

This article covers some of my thinking around:

  • Key Leadership Behaviours for successful implementation
  • Technical Leadership Considerations for curriculum planning
  • Prompt Questions to frame your school’s readiness
  • Resources and Next Steps to support your journey

1. Leadership Behaviours That Enable Change

Curriculum change is not simply a matter of compliance, but also cultural transformation. The evidence from implementation science, including the EEF’s guidance report Putting Evidence to Work, shows that leadership behaviours are critical in determining whether reforms become embedded or remain superficial.

Set Vision with Optimism

Effective change leadership begins with a compelling narrative. Leaders should frame the Review as an opportunity to deepen equity and raise ambition. For instance, the Review highlights how disadvantage manifests in reduced access to broad subjects, like MFL and computing, and proposes curriculum updates to address this.

  • How do we communicate the purpose and benefits of these changes to staff and families?
  • Do we have a clear, hopeful narrative that connects reform to our school values?

“The refreshed national curriculum must be an aspirational, engaging and demanding offer that reflects the high expectations and excellence our young people deserve, irrespective of background.”

Distribute Leadership

Evidence from the OECD and EEF underlines the importance of distributed leadership for long-term sustainability. Empower subject leads to take ownership of aspects of curriculum redesign, particularly where changes intersect with specialist knowledge (e.g. computing, Citizenship, oracy).

  • Which subject and phase leaders are best positioned to lead change?
  • How will we share accountability and recognition across the team?

Protect Time

Time is the most constrained resource in schools. Leaders must build structured time for curriculum redesign, team collaboration, and subject-specific CPD. This is especially vital where new / proposed frameworks (e.g. oracy, digital literacy, climate education) are unfamiliar and require sustained planning.

  • Do our calendars protect time for collaborative planning and review?
  • How can we repurpose existing meeting time to support curriculum development?

Model Inquiry and Adaptation

Use diagnostic tools and ongoing feedback to evaluate impact. For example, the proposed Year 8 English and Maths assessments will offer new data points. Leaders can model evidence-led decision making by analysing and acting on such data early.

  • What early indicators will show whether changes are improving outcomes?
  • How will we build feedback loops between teachers, subject leads, and SLT?

Build Buy-In and Resilience

Cultural change requires collaboration with care. Recognise staff anxieties, involve them early, and celebrate progress publicly. This is especially important in more disadvantaged school communities, where reform fatigue may be high.

  • How will we ensure staff feel valued and supported during implementation?
  • Are we celebrating progress as well as outcomes?

“The current system is not working well for all… Our recommendations aim to improve curriculum quality for all young people but will particularly benefit those for whom the system is currently not working well.”

2. Technical Leadership Considerations

Strategic and operational planning must consider both the scope of curriculum reform and the capacity of the system to deliver. Key technical steps include:

Map and Sequence Curriculum Updates

Review where your curriculum already aligns with the Review’s direction. For instance, do you teach computing to all pupils in KS4 (as the National Curriculum and the Review recommend)? Do you integrate media literacy and financial education meaningfully across key stages?

  • Which subjects or key stages need immediate attention?
  • How will we prioritise curriculum changes over a multi-year timeline?

Audit Time and Expertise

The Review notes subjects like languages and computing suffer from both limited curriculum time and staff shortages. Conduct a time audit by subject and identify departments needing additional allocation. Use this to inform CPD planning, recruitment, and scheduling.

  • Do our current allocations reflect the breadth required by the updated National Curriculum?
  • What is our plan to build subject expertise where shortages exist?

“80% of teachers from maintained schools report that Computing is taught at Key Stage 4 only to students taking GCSE Computer Science, while only around 10% say that Computing is taught to all Key Stage 4 students.”

Rebalance Depth and Breadth

One major finding of the Review is the need to ensure a better balance between breadth and mastery. Leaders should critically assess which content can be deprioritised to allow for deeper learning. Consider aligning schemes of work with clear progression models and reducing unnecessary repetition between phases.

  • Where is our curriculum overloaded / imbalanced?
  • How can we increase depth without reducing ambition?

Alignment with Ofsted’s EIF

The revised EIF from November 2025 makes clear that curriculum intent and implementation must reflect ambition for all pupils. Ofsted will expect schools to meet or exceed the expectations of the national curriculum. As subjects like primary Citizenship become statutory and computing becomes more universal to 16, leaders must be ready to explain and evidence their curriculum rationale.

  • How will our curriculum intent statement evolve to reflect new priorities?
  • How confident are subject leads in articulating curriculum ambition and progression?

Plan for Assessment Evolution: The Review outlines new diagnostic assessments and potential reforms at 16–19 (e.g. new Level 3 V Qualifications). Leaders should anticipate how these will affect internal tracking systems, assessment culture, and student pathways.

  • What systems do we need to track pupil progress through new diagnostic points?
  • How will these changes affect our options and careers guidance at KS4–5?

Navigating Workforce and Financial Pressures

Implementing major curriculum reform is not a one-term project. It will span years. Thus, one of the most effective leadership behaviours is persistence with a purpose. Keep the rationale alive (why we are doing this) and celebrate early wins. Did a new approach lead to better student engagement? Share that story in a staff briefing. Did a mid-year assessment show improvement in writing after a curriculum tweak? Let everyone know, as it boosts morale and buy-in. I know from experience that those who are resilient, patient, and committed to continuous improvement will find their school communities follow suit.

Teacher supply is a pressing concern. Some of the areas targeted by the Review coincide with subjects facing recruitment shortfalls. For example, in Modern Foreign Languages only 43% of the initial teacher training target was met in 2024/25 – a stark indicator of shortage. Computing, another spotlight subject, has struggled with inconsistent delivery partly due to “important issues of teacher supply” in that field. School and department leaders will need to get creative to meet new requirements: this could involve upskilling existing staff, sharing teachers across schools (via trusts or local networks), or leveraging external resources like the National Centre for Computing Education. The Review suggests using such national resources and specialist hubs to help implement changes where teacher expertise is lacking. Leaders of disadvantaged secondary schools, who often face higher staff turnover and fewer specialist teachers, should advocate for and take advantage of any additional support or funding streams the government provides alongside the reforms.

On the financial front, budgets are tight across the sector and new initiatives often require upfront investment (curriculum materials, training days, possibly hiring specialists). While the Review does not come with direct funding, it does recommend careful staging of implementation to mitigate burden. In fact, the report’s conclusion urges the Government to take a “careful, staged approach to implementation” that accounts for the wider challenges facing schools. School leaders can mirror this approach internally. Rather than attempting a wholesale switch overnight, it’s wise to prioritize changes and spread them over time, aligned with guidance from the Department for Education. For instance, if new programmes of study are being drafted in late 2025 with consultations in 2026, schools might pilot certain elements (like an oracy initiative or a revised Year 7 unit) in 2026–27, then ramp up once final curricula and exam specs are confirmed.

Another aspect of resource management is time; arguably a school’s most precious resource. Leaders will need to allocate sufficient meeting time, INSET days, and collaborative planning sessions for departments to digest the new curriculum content and redesign, adapt and contextualise schemes of work. The Review explicitly notes that some subjects suffer from minimal curriculum time in practice. If your school is in this boat, you may have to rebalance timetable hours to meet the heightened expectations (for example, ensuring computing and languages get adequate hours without sacrificing core subjects). These timetable choices are tough in any school, but particularly in disadvantaged areas where there may be pressure to focus on English and Maths. Nonetheless leaders might need to protect or expand time for foundation subjects in order to fulfil the curriculum’s breadth. This must be done while keeping an eye on staff workload pressures too.

3. (Likely) Next Steps for Leaders

The coming months are an ideal time to get ahead on planning and to engage with the support available. Here are some clear next steps for leaders and where to find more information:

Digest the Official Documentation

Read the Curriculum and Assessment Review’s Final Report (titled “Building a world-class curriculum for all”) in full, or at least its executive summary and conclusions. This will give you a deeper understanding of the rationale behind each recommendation. The full report is available on GOV.UK, and includes subject-by-subject chapters and an annex of all recommendations. Additionally, keep an eye out for the Government’s formal response to the Review, which is due to be published alongside the report. This will indicate which recommendations are accepted and likely timelines.

Map Out a Timeline

Based on the information in the report and Government announcements, sketch a multi-year timeline for implementation. For example, if new Programmes of Study drafting begins in late 2025 and a public consultation is set for 2026, you can expect updated curriculum requirements to be confirmed by late 2026. That suggests changes might hit classrooms by 2027 or later. Plot these key milestones and align your school’s internal preparation (e.g. “Summer 2026: train staff on new primary Citizenship content; Autumn 2026: update KS3 schemes of work for computing” etc.).

Conduct a Curriculum Audit

Perform an audit of your current curriculum and practices. Identify areas of strong alignment with the Review (these can be built upon and celebrated) and areas of risk or gap. For instance, audit Key Stage 4 offerings – are arts and technical subjects accessible to all, or have past accountability measures narrowed your offer? Also audit lesson time allocation per subject in each key stage and compare it with any new emphases (do you need to allocate more time to computing or languages?). This audit sets a baseline from which to plan improvements.

Engage and Train Staff

Hold initial briefings with your senior leadership team and then with middle leaders to share what the Review means for the school. Encourage departments to start informal gap analysis (some proactive subject leaders may already be aware of likely changes from their professional networks). Begin to identify professional development needs emerging from this (for example, teachers may need support in curriculum design skills or new subject knowledge). Look for resources from subject associations, exam boards, and DfE training offers.

The government is likely to support certain priority areas (for example, there are existing Language Hubs and Computing Hubs that might ramp up activity to help meet new requirements). Make use of these networks. Establish a professional learning community in your school for curriculum development, so that teachers can share tips and resources as they adapt plans.

Monitor Ongoing Developments

The policy landscape will continue to evolve as the Review’s proposals turn into action. Keep up with updates from the Department for Education and Ofsted. For example, Ofsted’s new inspection framework guidance (effective November 2025) is already published; review it to ensure your inspection preparations align with both existing expectations and future curriculum changes.

Watch for any pilot programs or funding announcements (the DfE might, for instance, pilot the Year 8 diagnostic assessments or provide grants for teacher training in shortage subjects). Consider nominating your school for pilot opportunities if available as being involved early can give you a head-start and a voice in shaping how policies are implemented.

Collaborate and Share Practice

Link up with other schools, especially those in similar circumstances (e.g. secondary schools with higher disadvantage) to share strategies. Many heads and trust leaders will be tackling the same issues. Through local clusters, academy trusts, or professional forums, you can pool expertise. Some schools might focus on specific innovations (for example, one school might develop an excellent model for primary financial literacy teaching – learn from them, while you might share your effective approach to, say, integrated STEM curriculum). Collaboration reduces burden and increases consistency, which ultimately benefits the system as a whole and eases transitions for pupils who move between schools.

4. Final Reflections

This curriculum reform is not just another initiative. For school leaders, this is both exciting and challenging. How to navigate it? Start now: audit your curriculum against the new recommendations. Are you as broad and deep as you want to be? Plan for workforce and training needs. Align these changes with the new Ofsted framework. Crucially, think about the rhythm: these changes won’t drop overnight. We expect a phased rollout over the next couple of years. Use that time to engage staff, update approaches and curriculum planning, and communicate with parents and governors about what’s coming.

“The socio-economic gap in relation to educational attainment remains stubbornly wide, and young people with special educational needs and disabilities make less progress than their peers.”

As with most education systems change, the quality of leadership will determine whether this opportunity delivers on its promise. With clarity, evidence, and compassion, school leaders can shape implementation in a way that is rigorous, realistic, and rooted in the needs of their pupils. In doing so, we will ensure that a richer, fairer, and more future-oriented education becomes the norm, not the exception.

Finally, be reassured that the Review panel believes the recommendations are “ambitious but achievable”. They explicitly advise that implementation be staged and considerate of real-world challenges – a message that school leaders can take to heart. It’s neither necessary nor wise to rush. By demonstrating strategic leadership – careful planning, inclusive practices, and a focus on sustainable improvement – your school can navigate these changes successfully, even under pressure.

A richer curriculum and fairer chances for all our young people will be worth all the hard work ahead. Let’s lead this well.
