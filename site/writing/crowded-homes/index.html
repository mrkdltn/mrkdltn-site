Poorer pupils do best where homes are most crowded. Housing isn’t why.

Across England’s councils, disadvantaged pupils get their best GCSE results where homes are most crowded. Read alongside this week’s Sutton Trust report and June’s Inquiry into white working class outcomes, the reason has little to do with housing, and the two reports call for different responses.

Mark DaltonFirst published on LinkedIn, 27 November 2026Sources

Two reports this year have shaped how we talk about disadvantage. This week the Sutton Trust's Home Truths found that housing accounts for about a tenth of the gap in GCSE results between poorer and better-off families, almost all of it through space and quiet to study.11Sutton Trust and UCL CEPEO, Home Truths, 26 November 2026, pp. 39–40: 10.2% of the association between household income and the Key Stage 4 point score, 9.5 points of it the physical home learning environment. Millennium Cohort Study, born 2000–02, England. In June the Independent Inquiry into White Working Class Educational Outcomes reported that only 36% of White British pupils on free school meals reach grade 4 in English and maths, half the rate of pupils not on free school meals.22Independent Inquiry into White Working Class Educational Outcomes, full report, June 2026, pp. 6–7: 36% against 72%, grade 4 or above in English and maths. Read alongside the official housing figures, the two reports largely describe different places, and partly different children. Poorer pupils do best in the councils where homes are most crowded, and the reason has little to do with housing. Housing matters, but it does not explain the White British gap.

What the Sutton Trust found

Home Truths, written by economists at UCL's Centre for Education Policy and Equalising Opportunities, follows children through three long-running studies. At 11, 52% of children in the poorest fifth of families shared a bedroom, against 7% in the richest fifth. Comparing otherwise similar young people, those whose home conditions were typical of the poorest fifth scored about a sixth of a grade lower in each of their best eight GCSEs. Their comparisons are between young people of the same ethnic group, so the report speaks to the income gap rather than to gaps between groups.33Home Truths, p. 63: models compare young people of the same age, gender and ethnic group, with similar household income and size, ability at age seven, region and tenure. The authors are clear that the tenth describes how the gap is made up, not how much of it would close if housing improved, and they call for housing and education records to be linked.

The report's cohorts sat their GCSEs before the pandemic. The government's figures, published the same morning, show where the pressure falls now.

Where the housing pressure falls

On 30 June, 178,690 children in England were in temporary accommodation.44MHCLG, Statutory homelessness in England: April to June 2026, published 26 November 2026, table TA1. Rates use ONS mid-2025 population estimates of children aged 0 to 17. Counted against the children who live in each area, that is about one for every two classes of 30 across England, and more than four for every 30 in Westminster and Newham. Twenty of the 22 highest councils are London boroughs; the exceptions are Slough and Birmingham.

Behind that total sit long stays, distant placements, bed and breakfast and crowding, and they fall on different councils. Westminster places 65% of its households in temporary accommodation outside its own borough. Birmingham holds nearly half of the families with children kept in bed and breakfast beyond the six-week legal limit. Far more children live in crowded homes than are ever counted as homeless. At the 2021 Census, 12.4% of households in Leicester and 15.8% in Slough had fewer bedrooms than they needed.55ONS, Census 2021, TS052 occupancy rating for bedrooms. Out-of-area placements from table TA9; bed and breakfast beyond six weeks from TA1.

Schools are usually the last to know where their pupils sleep. I have worked in schools in west London and Birmingham, and now lead one in Warwickshire, and it has been true in each. We tend to learn that a family is in temporary accommodation once something else has started to slip: a run of late marks, homework that stops arriving, a pupil who is tired before the day begins. By then the move may be weeks old. What has helped most has rarely been a programme. It has been one adult who knows the family's situation, a quiet room open after school, and the judgement to treat lateness from a long journey across a city as a housing problem rather than a behaviour one.

Who lives in crowded homes

Crowding is not evenly shared. In the government's survey figures for April 2020 to March 2023, 2% of White British households were overcrowded, the lowest rate of any ethnic group, against 18% of Bangladeshi and 16% of Black African households. In every income band, White British households were less likely to be crowded than households from all other ethnic groups combined.66Ethnicity facts and figures, Overcrowded households, English Housing Survey April 2020 to March 2023, updated 5 September 2025.

That explains a pattern that looks wrong at first. Across 127 unitary, metropolitan and London councils, the most crowded places have the best results for disadvantaged pupils. They are also the places where fewest disadvantaged pupils are White British, a group whose results are lower on average nationally; the two measures run opposite, with a rank correlation of −0.94.77DfE, Key stage 4 performance 2025/26: local authority district data by pupil residency, and pupil characteristics by local authority (ethnicity and disadvantage). Spearman rank correlations across council areas. Compare White British disadvantaged pupils with one another and the crowded places no longer look better. Across all councils there is no relationship, and outside London more crowded places tend to have lower results.

The gap in every kind of place

The Inquiry describes a gap that opens early and widens through school, with lower attendance and a weaker sense of belonging. Around two in five white working class pupils, it reports, are persistently absent.88Inquiry full report, p. 39 (38% of white pupils on free school meals persistently absent); housing appears in Recommendation 01, pp. 56–57. Housing appears once in its full report, in a call to add measures such as housing to the national education datasets, the same gap in the data that Home Truths identifies.

The council figures let me test whether crowding accounts for the White British gap. Outside London, in the quarter of councils where homes are least crowded, White British disadvantaged pupils averaged 32.1 in Attainment 8 this summer, against an estimated 38.8 for other disadvantaged pupils. In the most crowded quarter, the figures were 29.0 and 41.1. Other disadvantaged pupils do broadly similarly across all four groups of councils, between 38.8 and 41.1; White British disadvantaged pupils do somewhat less well where homes are more crowded, and trail everywhere. Seven points where homes are least crowded, twelve where they are most. The gap is there in both.

Because White British families are less likely than any other group to live in crowded homes, crowding is unlikely to explain why White British disadvantaged pupils do less well than other disadvantaged pupils. It may still explain part of their gap with better-off classmates, as it does for every group. In these area data, crowding and diversity move together too closely to tell their effects apart, and nothing here shows that any group's presence affects another's results. The Inquiry's account of attendance and belonging may fit the pattern better, though these data cannot test it.

This also echoes my analysis of 3,256 schools last week, which found that the White British pattern held in affluent neighbourhoods as well as deprived ones. Here it holds in crowded and uncrowded places alike.

Space and quiet would help a great many children. On their own, they would not close the White British gap. So the two reports call for different responses, often in different places. Children in crowded and temporary homes, from every background and disproportionately from some minority ethnic groups, need space and quiet to study, and a stable home. White British disadvantaged pupils need what the Inquiry describes, wherever they live. Some children need both.

The one trace schools can see

School data record one consequence of housing insecurity: a late change of school. In 2025/26, 27,672 pupils in England joined their school during Year 10 or 11, and 36.5% of them achieved grade 4 or above in English and maths, against 66.2% of other pupils.99DfE, Key stage 4 performance 2025/26, pupil characteristics: mobility. DfE counts a pupil as mobile if they joined in the last two academic years. Outside London, councils with more children in temporary accommodation per child also have more late joiners.

Pupils who arrive in Year 10 or 11 test whether a school's systems were built with them in mind. In my experience the first fortnight matters most: a reading check and a conversation about what they were studying before, courses chosen around what they can realistically complete, and an adult who checks in with them every day at first. What I would change is how their results are reported. A pupil on roll at the January census of Year 11 counts in full in the receiving school's results, however late in the autumn they arrived, which quietly rewards schools that find reasons not to take them.

What the data cannot tell us

Every comparison here is between areas, and none of it shows cause. Ethnicity stands in for much else, including migration history, language and neighbourhood, and the White British families who live in the most diverse towns may differ from those elsewhere. Crowding is measured across all households in an area, not in these pupils' homes, and the national survey of crowding by ethnicity covers all households, not only poorer families with children. The "other disadvantaged" figures are estimated by subtraction across two Department for Education datasets with slightly different bases. The Inquiry's 36% uses free school meal eligibility, while the council figures use the wider disadvantaged measure. The Department for Education counts a pupil as a late joiner if they arrived in the last two academic years, from mid-September of Year 10. Children in temporary accommodation are counted by the council that placed them, while population is counted where children live. The figures come from different dates: mid-2025 population, June 2026 temporary accommodation, the 2021 Census and the 2020–23 English Housing Survey.

What should change

  1. Back the flag, and publish what it shows. Home Truths proposes a flag in school data for children placed in temporary accommodation, like the one for care-experienced pupils, with extra support attached. I agree. My addition is that the Department for Education publishes the results and attendance of flagged pupils each year, as it does for children in care, so that both reports' call for housing in education data becomes something the system can act on.
  2. Make the new duty follow the child. Since 1 September, housing authorities must tell a child's school when the family is first placed in temporary accommodation.1010MHCLG, Temporary accommodation notification duty, 1 September 2026 (Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026). The duty applies once, at the first placement; later moves, including those to another area, are notified only by agreement. My proposal is that every move triggers a notice, to the current school and to the admissions team where the family now lives.
  3. Put late joiners on the front page. The Department for Education already publishes school-level results by mobility in its data files. My proposal is that performance tables and report cards show, beside the headline figures, how many pupils joined during Year 10 or 11 and how they did.
  4. Fund space and quiet where crowding is worst. My proposal is to fund after-school study space where homes are most crowded, including places where few families are in temporary accommodation.
  5. Read both gaps together. School-level data break results down by disadvantage but not by ethnicity. The department has calculated school-level scores for disadvantaged White British pupils once, for 2023/24, and published only national counts of schools. My proposal is that it publishes disadvantaged pupils' results by ethnicity for each school, suppressed where numbers are small, in the spirit of the Inquiry's call for a clearer definition of "white working class", so that schools serving mixed communities can see both gaps at once.

Heads in diverse towns outside London: how do your White British disadvantaged pupils do compared with your other disadvantaged pupils, and what do you think explains the difference?
